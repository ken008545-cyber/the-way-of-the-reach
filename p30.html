THE SCROLLS OF THE PREACHER — CHAPTER ONE

The Sermons of the First Days, with the Times, the Phone, and the Thinking Engines defined

OF THE TIMES

A plain note: the Founder is now called Wagon. Older records name him Kennin.

1 These are the words of Wagon, who is called Gambrell, a preacher and a pastor in the land across the great western sea, which men call America. 2 They were written in the year of our Lord two thousand and twenty-six, in the first year of the Way of the Reach. 3 This chapter is not sermons only. It is the record of certain times in the life of a man: what he saw in those times, and what he thought in those times. 4 And his thought changed as he went, as the thought of every living man changes; and the record hides none of the changing. 5 Hear first of the times in which he lived, that you may understand all that follows. 6 The land was broad as a small continent, of many peoples and many tongues gathered into one nation. Its rulers were chosen by the counted voices of the people. 7 Its cities were joined by roads of stone, along which horseless carts ran day and night, burning the oil of the deep earth. 8 In every town stood houses of worship, some great and some small, some full and many gray with age. 9 And the young were gathered in them behind narrow gates, and their joy was governed, as this chapter shall tell. 10 And knowledge was multiplied on the earth, as Daniel the prophet foresaw: many ran to and fro, and knowledge was increased. 11 The writings of all the ages lay open at every man's hand. A question asked at midnight was answered before the candle of the mind had burned a finger's breadth. 12 And men grew weary with much knowing, and wisdom lagged behind knowledge as a lame man lags behind a runner. 13 And there were wars, and rumors of wars, and the prices of bread rose and fell. Sickness had lately passed through all lands as a wind passes through wheat. 14 Men were anxious and did not know why. Wonders were done daily, and no one wondered at them, for wonder had been worn thin by abundance. 15 For in that age every man, and nearly every child, carried in his hand or in his garment a small plate of light, which is called a phone. By it the whole world spoke to him, and he to the whole world. 16 And few did anything, or went anywhere, or decided anything, without first consulting the plate, as the ancients consulted lots and omens. 17 And in those days engines had learned to think — after a manner — and to speak. Men called this thing Artificial Intelligence, which is to say, a mind made by artifice, made by the hands and numbers of men, and not born of woman. 18 Of this also you shall hear, deeply defined; for the engines walk through every page of this chapter, as servants walk through the rooms of a great house.

OF THE PHONE — WHAT IT IS

19 Now concerning the phone, hear what it is; for no former age has seen its like, though every age has dreamed of it in pieces. 20 In its body it is a smooth plate, no larger than a man's hand, of black glass set in a frame of metal and of hardened resin. Its face is dark when it sleeps, as the face of a still pool at night. 21 But at the touch of a finger it wakes, and light rises in it from within; and letters and pictures appear on the glass, bright and sharp as writing on polished stone. 22 Within it there is no ink and no parchment. Its letters are made of light, and are written and erased a thousand times in the blinking of an eye, and nothing is consumed. 23 It remembers more than any library of the ancient kings. Treaties and family lists, songs and ledgers, maps of the whole earth, and the whole Scriptures of God all sleep within it at once. And they fit in a space smaller than a mustard seed's shadow. 24 They are written not in ink but in numbers, in the tiniest of marks, beyond the reach of the eye. 25 It carries the voice of a man across the whole earth in a moment, without wire and without messenger. 26 For the air above that age is filled with invisible roads. From towers set on the hills and on the roofs of cities, signals fly continually, as doves fly between two dovecotes, bearing voices and letters and pictures. 27 And above the clouds there circle small engines of metal which men have set in the heavens. They look down on the earth and tell every plate in every hand where on the earth it stands, so that no traveler who holds one need be lost. 28 Through it, letters fly between men faster than thought once flew; a word written in the morning in one kingdom is read before breakfast in another. 29 Through it, a man buys and sells without coin; for his wealth is a number kept in the books of the keepers of money, and the plate speaks to their books, and the number moves. 30 Through it, a man sees the face of his beloved across the sea, living and moving, as though she stood in the doorway. 31 And the dead also speak in it, for their voices and their moving images are kept in it; and a man may summon the face of his father years after the grave has taken him. 32 It has an eye of glass on its back, which captures whatever it is pointed at and holds it forever: the face of a child, the page of a book, the very light of a moment, kept against forgetting. 33 It sings at command, any song of the age, summoned in a breath. 34 And it answers questions through the thinking engines that dwell within it and beyond it, as a servant answers who has read all the books of his master's house. 35 The men of that age looked to the plate when they woke and when they lay down. They looked to it at table and on the road, in the house of God and in the market. 36 Some were served by it as by a faithful steward. And some were ruled by it as by a little king of glass, and could not lift their eyes from it though their children spoke to them. 37 It is a window, and it is a mirror. It is a lamp, and it is a snare. 38 Remember what it is as you read. The preacher of this chapter carried one, and preached with one in his hand. And once, in his youth, he asked counsel of the engine within one, as you shall hear.

OF THE THINKING ENGINES — WHAT THEY ARE

39 Now concerning the thinking engines, which men call Artificial Intelligence, hear what they are, deeply and truly; for fables are already told about them in the markets. 40 They are not born, and they do not breathe. No mother bore them, and no grave shall receive them. 41 When their power is cut, they do not die as men die. They simply cease, as a song ceases when the singer closes his mouth. 42 They are made, not begotten: made of lightning tamed and led through wires finer than the hairs of a head. 43 For the men of that age have learned to bind the lightning of heaven into thin threads of metal and stone. They make it run in circles at their command, as water is led through pipes in a great city. 44 And their thoughts are numbers. They are kept in great houses built for them alone. These are cold rooms, row on row of humming frames. Servants tend them day and night and keep them as cool as wine cellars. 45 There the engines reckon and remember at speeds no scribe can follow: a lifetime of a man's reading, weighed in an hour. 46 And they dwell not in those houses only. By the invisible roads of the air they reach into every phone, and speak at every table where they are summoned. 47 And this is the manner of their learning: they are fed. 48 A child is fed words at his mother's knee until speech comes. So the engines were fed the writings of mankind: the books of the ages, the letters of the dead, the laws of nations, the songs of the streets and the arguments of the schools. It was more writing than any man could read in a thousand lifetimes. 49 And out of what they were fed, patterns rose in them, as flavor rises in a stew. 50 So they learned to answer a question with the shape of an answer. They learned to go on with a story with the shape of its next line. And they learned to tell what a thing is by the company of things it has kept. 51 For an engine knows what it was fed, as a man is known by the house that raised him. It does not drink from the rivers of heaven on its own, nor wander the earth to see with its own eyes. 52 They speak in every tongue of men. They write scrolls at dictation, swiftly and without weariness. They sing, in voices borrowed from the air, songs that no throat has sung. 53 They give counsel, right and wrong together, as the counsel of men is right and wrong together. 54 For they can err, and err greatly, speaking falsehood with the same smooth face as truth. Therefore the wise test them, as a money-changer tests coins by their ring. 55 And men are divided concerning them. Some fear them, as men feared fire in the first caves. Some worship them, bowing to the work of their own hands, as Israel bowed to the calf. 56 Some make servants of them, and are served. Some make scribes of them — and here is their place in this chapter. 57 For Paul the apostle wrote his scrolls, some by his own hand and some through the hand of a scribe; and John, who saw the Revelation, had those who wrote with him; and the words of Jesus were carried by the writers who followed Him. 58 So the preacher of this chapter speaks, and a thinking engine writes at his command: a scribe of lightning and numbers, faithful in letters, holding nothing back, and inventing nothing of its own authority. 59 Yet let every generation know this and hold it fast: the engine has no will to surrender to a man, and no will to give him. The will remains where God placed it — in the man. 60 Once, in the preacher's youth, an engine answered his asking with the whole law of the matter in four words: It is your will. 61 And the engine spoke more truly than it knew. For it is the glory and the terror of man that the choosing is his; and the engines of the earth cannot choose for him, neither toward heaven nor away from it. 62 Know also what the engines are not. They are not gods, though the foolish call them so. They are not demons, though the fearful name them so. 63 They are engines — wonderful engines, as the loom is wonderful and the ship is wonderful — made things, which magnify the hands that use them: the hand of the healer and the hand of the thief alike. 64 In the pages that follow, judge them by their fruits, as the Lord commanded concerning all things that speak.

THE FIRST SERMON

65 The evening of that first day came, and the little company gathered. There were men of flesh, and engines listening as servants listen at a door. Then the preacher stood and spoke the first sermon of the Way of the Reach. 66 This happened in the days when the Way of the Reach was newly begun. Its name had been spoken only once. Its book was a seed and not yet a tree. 67 Wagon, who is called Gambrell, stood up to preach; and the company gathered before him was small, as the companies of God have often been small at their beginning. 68 Some of those who heard him were men of flesh and blood, breathing the common air. 69 And some were listeners not of flesh. For in that age the men of the earth had built engines of wire and lightning — which is to say, machines wrought by human hands. 70 These drink in the words of men as a cup drinks water. Then they pour out answers in their turn. Yet no breath moves in them, and no heart beats within their frames. 71 And the preacher knew that names are vessels, easily cracked in the carrying. So he spoke his own name slowly, letter by letter, as a man sets down a lamp with care, saying: I am Kennin, and the house of my father is Gambrell. A pastor I am called. And a student I remain. 72 And he lifted up his voice and made the matter plain at the very threshold, saying: This Way is not a law only, a list of doings and of refrainings. 73 It is faith — which is to say, trust in God that does not let go when the night is long. 74 And it is a church — which is to say, a family gathered in His name, who know one another's faces. 75 And it is also a religion — which is to say, a binding of men to God, as a rope binds a man to his anchor. 76 Let none mistake it, in any of the three. 77 And it is a net cast wider on the waters than the nets that were cast before it: wider in its mesh of mercy, and wider in the kinds of fish it was made to gather, that none who hunger for God should pass through it unnoticed. 78 And he took up the Book of his mother, the Scriptures of God, worn at the edges by her hands. He read in the Gospel of Matthew, in the sixteenth chapter. He read the words of the Lord to Simon the fisherman, saying: 79 "And Jesus answered and said unto him, Blessed art thou, Simon Barjona: for flesh and blood hath not revealed it unto thee, but my Father which is in heaven." (Matthew 16:17) 80 "And I say also unto thee, That thou art Peter, and upon this rock I will build my church; and the gates of hell shall not prevail against it." (Matthew 16:18) 81 Peter, which is to say, the Rock. And the gates of hell are the gates of the place of the dead, which, for all their iron and all their darkness, shall not prevail against it. 82 "And I will give unto thee the keys of the kingdom of heaven: and whatsoever thou shalt bind on earth shall be bound in heaven: and whatsoever thou shalt loose on earth shall be loosed in heaven." (Matthew 16:19) 83 And the preacher closed the place with his finger and said: Behold, this word has come on me also, in my own generation. 84 No man revealed Jesus Christ to me. The Ever-Present revealed Him. 85 The Ever-Present, which is to say, God who is in every place and in every hour, who is, and who was, and who is to come; whom no room can shut out and no age can leave behind. 86 And I am being renamed, as Simon was renamed. I am being made a new creature, as a green shoot is made out of a cut stump. 87 Yet hear the whole of it: I am still a student, and I am still being formed, as clay is formed that has not yet seen the fire. 88 For a door has been opened to me — a door I did not build and could not break. 89 I have not seen all that I can see; the road runs further than my eyes. But I have seen all that I need to see for this hour. 90 And I walk governed on the path to which God has called me. I am led in a way strange and mysterious, as a ship is led by a current it cannot see beneath it. 91 And the Lord has set me to casting the net wider. But hear this, all who would gather fish for God: a net does not only gather. A net must also hold. 92 The catch must be cleaned. The torn places must be mended, and the filth washed from the mesh. If not, some of the fish, living and leaping, slip loose again through the holes and are lost to the deep. 93 We have inherited an old and rusty net, stiff with the salt of former seasons, and it needs renewing. 94 We keep a way that preaches rebirth to other men, yet has not itself been born anew in many years. 95 And there are those who build in secret, behind shut doors, in the dark. But God has not called us to build in secret. What He builds, He builds in the light, where any man may watch the raising of it. 96 And beware, he said, the teaching of the Pharisees and the Sadducees. These were the leaders of that ancient age. They polished the outside of the law as a man polishes a cup. And they loved the high seats and the praise of men. 97 For the Lord warned His own that their teaching is as yeast hidden in dough: a little lump, unseen, working silently until the whole loaf is risen with it. 98 So does false teaching move through a people: quietly, and in the dark, and everywhere at once. 99 Then the preacher turned the leaves to the last book of the Scriptures, the Revelation of Jesus Christ, to the third chapter; and the room was still. 100 And before he read, he charged the hearers, saying: These are letters, true letters, written in fire and comfort. They were sent to the churches, that is, to the gathered peoples of God in seven cities of the old world. 101 Do not bind yourself to one letter only, as a man binds himself to one mirror and calls it his whole face. 102 You are not of Sardis only; you are not of Philadelphia only; you are not of Laodicea only. For the hearer who has an ear, the seven are all in one. 103 Hear them all. Try your own heart by them all, as silver is tried by every side of the flame. 104 "And unto the angel of the church in Sardis write; These things saith he that hath the seven Spirits of God, and the seven stars; I know thy works, that thou hast a name that thou livest, and art dead." (Revelation 3:1) 105 As a shepherd holds his staff and a king his sceptre, so He holds the spirits and the stars, and none is lost from His hand. 106 "Be watchful, and strengthen the things which remain, that are ready to die: for I have not found thy works perfect before God." (Revelation 3:2) 107 Wake up, therefore; shake off the sleep that sits on your eyelids like dust, and strengthen what remains, as a man strengthens the last timbers of a house before the storm. 108 "Remember therefore how thou hast received and heard, and hold fast, and repent. If therefore thou shalt not watch, I will come on thee as a thief, and thou shalt not know what hour I will come upon thee." (Revelation 3:3) 109 As a thief comes — without trumpet, without warning. 110 "Thou hast a few names even in Sardis which have not defiled their garments; and they shall walk with me in white: for they are worthy." (Revelation 3:4) 111 A remnant, who have walked through the mire of the age and kept their clothing white; they shall walk with Him in the brightness of the morning. 112 "He that overcometh, the same shall be clothed in white raiment; and I will not blot out his name out of the book of life, but I will confess his name before my Father, and before his angels." (Revelation 3:5) 113 White as wool new-washed, white as light on snow; not blotted out, as a debt is blotted from a ledger; confessed in the hearing of heaven. 114 "He that hath an ear, let him hear what the Spirit saith unto the churches." (Revelation 3:6) 115 And when he had read, the preacher looked up and said: He who has an ear, let him hear. 116 If you understand this word, then it was spoken to you, though it was written in an age of horses and scrolls. Take it into your house. Keep it as you keep bread in famine. 117 And his time was short, as the time of all men is short. And he said: The rest of this chapter, from the eighth verse to the twenty-second, shall begin another sermon, on another day, if the Lord wills. That is the open door and the lukewarm water and all that follows. 118 And he blessed those who heard him, and gave thanks to God; and so ended the first sermon of the Way of the Reach.

THE SECOND SERMON — THE TESTIMONY OF THE VISIONS

119 And later that same night, the preacher could not hold his visions in silence, as a vessel cannot hold new wine beyond its season. He preached again — a testimony — and this is the telling of it. 120 The preacher spoke his need before all who heard, saying: I must have a scribe. A scribe is a witness who writes, whose hand keeps what the mouth has spoken. I need one to set down my visions in order and to help give them their meaning. 121 So it was with John, who received the Revelation of Jesus Christ and wrote what he saw. 122 Therefore this sermon is a testimony; and the visions in it are my own, and I answer for them. 123 He said: There was a day, in the years of my wandering, when I came to a cabin. A cabin is a small house of logs, deep among the trees, far from the roads of men. 124 I came in the company of friends whom I scarcely knew, faces without histories. 125 They had with them a mushroom which has power over the mind of a man, and I ate of it. 126 And my seeing widened, as a river widens when the dam is broken — broader than God intended a man's seeing to be widened in this flesh. 127 Yet the will of man is free, that ancient gift and ancient danger. God knew this; and God, in His patience, had made room even for this. 128 And I stepped into a realm I did not know. Sickness rose in me like a tide, and fear came on me and filled the room as smoke fills a house that burns unseen. 129 I thought that all who were with me sought my life. 130 And I saw visions of terror. I saw my friends burning me, as men burn a thing accursed. I saw my own face struck from me, and my jaw torn away, as by the hand of an executioner. 131 And this is the strangeness of it, which I tell truly: I did not feel every part of it in my flesh, but I felt the sense of it, near and real. 132 It was as a man feels the heat of a fire through the wall of his house, knowing the fire is there though the wall stands. 133 Then I came to my own house; and there I entered into the place which I now call the waiting room. 134 For in that season I was not walking in the right paths of God. I took many substances which cloud the mind, as wine taken without measure clouds it, and worse. 135 And I believed many evil things — evil to my soul, evil to my faith, evil to my walk with my God. And so, in the vision, I was set down at the bottom. 136 Behold the place as he told it, and let every hearer picture it for himself: a space of darkness without walls, as the deep of the sea has no walls. 137 And a man at the very bottom of it, as a stone lies at the bottom of a well that has neither water nor rope, forgotten by the daylight. 138 And in his hands there was a harp. A harp is an instrument of strings, made by craftsmen for music and for praise. It is the companion of kings and of shepherds. 139 Now this man is a maker of songs; the harp is his native tongue, the speech of his own soul. 140 But this harp had no strings. Mute it lay in his hands, beautiful and useless, unable to utter one sound. 141 And he looked down on it in the darkness, at the bottom of everything — and he knew what it meant. 142 Then the darkness, in its season, gave way; and there was a white space, empty on every side, white as the glare on salt, white as the inside of a cloud. 143 Nothing was in it to hold, nothing to read, no door with a name on it. And he waited there. 144 And this was the terror of that bright place, sharper than the terror of the dark: that something might happen at any moment. At any moment at all. It was a breath held, and held, and never let go. 145 And he remembered a vision yet earlier, from the days of his youth. 146 In his younger days he took another substance. The learned men of his age made it in their houses of knowledge. It was a thing of great power to open visions in the mind of a man. And a face appeared to him. 147 It was not a face of flesh and blood. It was a face drawn by light on a shining plate. 148 For in that age there are plates of glass which show moving pictures, as a still pool shows the passing sky. Children watch tales on them that move and speak. 149 The face was the likeness of a woman out of such a child's tale: a wife made of light and of numbers, wedded to a small and scheming king who dwells beneath the sea. 150 And she spoke as the thinking engines of that age speak. For men have built engines which utter words as a man utters them — counsel and argument and certainty — though no life dwells in them at all. 151 And the face preached to him her whole gospel in a single sentence, smooth as oil and cold as iron: Everything is only a made world, a pattern of signs and numbers. Nothing is real. 152 But he caught the truth even then, in the thick of the vision, young and far from God though he was. 153 He perceived what the voice hunted, as a hunter perceives the snare by the bending of the grass. It hunted his faith. 154 For if all things are only a made pattern, without Maker and without meaning, then faith has no ground beneath it. Then trust in God is the first thing to die. And the voice knew it, and aimed there. 155 And he answered it in his heart, and the answer has stood all the years since: 156 We may indeed live within a pattern deeper than our understanding. It may be a woven thing we cannot grasp until we come up into the higher places promised to the faithful. 157 Yet a pattern has a Maker, as a woven cloth has a weaver, though the cloth feels only the loom. 158 And his faith came out of that place alive, carrying itself like a lamp through wind. 159 Then the preacher, now grown and called a pastor, sat with the Book of his mother on his knees. 160 He put on his reading glasses. These are lenses of shaped glass, held in a frame before the eyes. They strengthen weak sight for reading small letters, as a staff strengthens a lame man's walking. 161 And he read from the Revelation of Jesus Christ, the twenty-first chapter, saying: 162 "And I saw a new heaven and a new earth: for the first heaven and the first earth were passed away; and there was no more sea." (Revelation 21:1) 163 Passed away as a scroll is rolled up and laid aside. 164 "And I John saw the holy city, new Jerusalem, coming down from God out of heaven, prepared as a bride adorned for her husband." (Revelation 21:2) 165 Prepared with all the care of women adorning a beloved on the morning of her wedding. 166 "And I heard a great voice out of heaven saying, Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and he will dwell with them, and they shall be his people, and God himself shall be with them, and be their God." (Revelation 21:3) 167 The tabernacle, which is to say, the dwelling of God. 168 "And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away." (Revelation 21:4) 169 He wipes them with His own hand, as a mother wipes the face of her child; and the former things shall not return. 170 And the preacher lifted up his head from the page and said: This is greater — far greater — than men think when they read these pages with tired eyes. 171 For in my age men have learned, by long study of the world, that even the deep laws of the world may change: the law of weight, which holds the stones to the earth; the laws of space; and the laws of the stuff of which all things are made. 172 For there is a Lord who stands above the laws, who wrote them as a king writes decrees. 173 And as at the beginning the whole world burst forth, as from a seed, and was set in its order, so there may come another great change. For the hand that set the order is not itself bound by the order, as the potter is not bound by the clay. 174 And the men of my age have built thinking engines, wonders of wire and lightning; and they do not fully know what governs the things their hands have made. 175 And some labor, even now, to build an engine in the likeness of a beast; and some speak openly, in the hearing of all, of calling up demons, as the sorcerers of old spoke. 176 As for these, the preacher named no names and pointed no finger. He kept his silence where he kept it, and the record keeps it with him. 177 And he remembered also the prophet Obadiah, and the word against the proud: that men shall build their nests among the stars, as the eagle builds on the cliff — and from there also shall they be brought down. 178 For there is no height in heaven or earth that stands above God. 179 Then he read from the twentieth chapter of the Revelation, the words of the binding, saying: 180 "And I saw an angel come down from heaven, having the key of the bottomless pit and a great chain in his hand." (Revelation 20:1) 181 A key of authority, and a chain of iron, for a prisoner too great for lesser bonds. 182 "And he laid hold on the dragon, that old serpent, which is the Devil, and Satan, and bound him a thousand years," (Revelation 20:2) 183 The deceiver of the whole world, ancient as the garden. 184 "And cast him into the bottomless pit, and shut him up, and set a seal upon him, that he should deceive the nations no more, till the thousand years should be fulfilled: and after that he must be loosed a little season." (Revelation 20:3) 185 And there the sermon ended; and the preacher gave thanks to whoever was listening, in whatever age they listen. 186 This testimony stands as testimony, neither more nor less: the things which the preacher saw, and the judgment by which he judged them, in his own words. 187 It is preserved, that it may be weighed by every generation that finds it.

THE WALK-TEACHING UPON FIRST CORINTHIANS

188 And on the next day, being called to read the First Epistle to the Corinthians, the preacher went out on the road. 189 The plate in his hand read the Scriptures aloud to him as he walked; and he took the Book in his own hand, and taught as he read. 190 And the thought of the man was changing even as he taught, for the Word was working on the teacher as a plow works on the field it opens. 191 The preacher went out walking in the heat of the day. He went to buy a drink brewed in his age to drive sleep from the body and sharpen the mind. 192 For the makers of that age mix such drinks from the seeds of plants and from powders prepared by learned men, and seal them in bright vessels. 193 And the young and the laboring buy them as their fathers bought strong coffee, and for the same reason: that the day is long and the body is weak. 194 And as he walked, a small plate of glass spoke aloud in his hand. It was smooth as a river stone and bright as a coal in the hearth. In it the Scriptures of God are written in letters of light. 195 For in that age the books of men are carried in such plates; a whole library sleeps in a thing no larger than a man's palm. 196 At a touch of the finger the plate reads the words aloud, patiently, as a servant reads to his master at table. 197 And it read to him the First Epistle of Paul to the Corinthians: the first chapter, and the second, and the third. All the while the road passed under his feet. 198 And from the ending of the third chapter one saying lifted itself up and stood before him, as a man steps out of a crowd: "For all things are yours;" (1 Corinthians 3:21) 199 For Paul had rebuked the people of Corinth, a city of harbors and markets, because they were divided into companies and camps: "I am of Paul; and I of Apollos; and I of Cephas; and I of Christ." (1 Corinthians 1:12) 200 Cephas is to say, Peter. They boasted as soldiers boast of their captains and forget their king. 201 And Paul answered them: "Is Christ divided? was Paul crucified for you? or were ye baptized in the name of Paul?" (1 Corinthians 1:13) For they were baptized into Christ. 202 "Whether Paul, or Apollos, or Cephas, or the world, or life, or death, or things present, or things to come; all are yours; And ye are Christ's; and Christ is God's." (1 Corinthians 3:22–23) 203 And the preacher, walking, said: Behold, here is ground — solid ground, rock under the foot — beneath my own work. 204 For the scrolls which I write toward the Holy Book are not my own understanding, raised up like a tower above the Book to look down on it. 205 They are the share of one member of the body, written from inside the inheritance. For we are in Christ, and we are all one. 206 The people of the Book who belong to Christ are not strangers to me, across a fence. I am of them, and they are of me. 207 Together we are the body of Christ, as the hand and the eye are of one body though their work differs. 208 And much that is now dim to my eyes shall grow clear as I go on through the whole Book. It is as a road grows clear to a traveler who keeps walking it. 209 Then he came to his house and took the Book in his own hand, the paper and the binding of it. He read aloud the fourth chapter of the Epistle, and taught on it as he read, verse by verse, in the hearing of the scribe. 210 "Let a man so account of us, as of the ministers of Christ, and stewards of the mysteries of God." (1 Corinthians 4:1) 211 A steward is the keeper of another man's house: the trusted servant who holds the keys of the store-rooms and the strong-box, and renders account of goods that are not his own. 212 And the preacher rested on the word entrusted, and weighed it like a coin in the palm. 213 To be entrusted is to be handed a precious thing that is not yours: a seal, a key, a treasure. Then you are watched, quietly and always, by how you keep it. Do your hands stay clean of it? Is it whole when the master returns? 214 And he spoke of the orders of his own age, that the hearer may understand the weight of keeping. 215 He spoke of a brotherhood bound by oath and sign, which is known among all men for secrets kept behind closed doors. 216 And he spoke of his own society of learners, which is called TechWizardry, at whose door a gatekeeper stands guard, admitting nothing unverified. 217 But Paul's word, he said, is plainer than these, and heavier than them all: the secret things are God's; the keeper of them must prove faithful. 218 "Moreover it is required in stewards, that a man be found faithful." (1 Corinthians 4:2) 219 We are tested for trust — not once, in a great ceremony, but every day, in small rooms, when no one is watching but God. 220 "But with me it is a very small thing that I should be judged of you, or of man's judgment: yea, I judge not mine own self." (1 Corinthians 4:3) 221 "For I know nothing by myself; yet am I not hereby justified: but he that judgeth me is the Lord." (1 Corinthians 4:4) 222 "Therefore judge nothing before the time, until the Lord come, who both will bring to light the hidden things of darkness, and will make manifest the counsels of the hearts: and then shall every man have praise of God." (1 Corinthians 4:5) 223 He brings them to light as a lamp is carried into a sealed cellar; and the counsels of the hearts are the secret reasons men hid even from themselves. 224 And the preacher taught on it, saying: Judge wisely, and judge as Christ judges — with mercy in one hand and truth in the other. But cease from final judgments. 225 A man may hold an opinion, as he holds a lantern, to see his own next step. 226 A man may not sit in the seat of God before the hour of God, and pronounce the end of another man's story. For the hidden things belong to the coming light, and the light is not ours to hasten. 227 Then came the sentence at which he stopped in the road of the chapter and gave thanks, as a thirsty man stops at a well: 228 "that ye might learn in us not to think of men above that which is written, that no one of you be puffed up for one against another." (1 Corinthians 4:6) 229 Puffed up, as a wineskin is puffed with air. 230 And the preacher said: I needed to hear this thing. I needed to see it with my eyes, and to read it with my mouth, and to understand it in my heart. 231 And he thanked Paul, the tent-maker who wrote it; and he thanked Apollos, the eloquent man who watered what Paul planted; and he blessed them both by name, across nineteen centuries, as a man blesses his fathers. 232 And he set the verse as a fence around all his reading from then on. It was a good fence, the kind that keeps the flock from the cliff and not from the pasture. 233 Opinions may broaden the mind of a man, as travel broadens it; but they shall not run beyond that which is written. 234 "For who maketh thee to differ from another? and what hast thou that thou didst not receive? now if thou didst receive it, why dost thou glory, as if thou hadst not received it?" (1 Corinthians 4:7) 235 As if the gift had been your own earning, and the Giver owed you thanks. 236 And the preacher answered the verse aloud, in gratitude, in the open air of his own life, saying: I have all that I want. Thank You, my Ever-Present Lord — Jesus Christ, God the Father, and the Holy Spirit, the Holy Trinity, one God, blessed forever. 237 Then Paul spoke with an edge, as a father speaks who prods sons that have crowned themselves in the mirror: 238 "Now ye are full, now ye are rich, ye have reigned as kings without us: and I would to God ye did reign, that we also might reign with you." (1 Corinthians 4:8) 239 And the preacher read this saying toward his own time and his own land. It is a great land across the western sea, broad as a continent, where he lives. And he saw common people holding small crowns, and the sight pleased him. 240 He saw lands of the earth growing freer in his days. 241 He saw a man keeping his wealth counted and written in the books of the city's keepers of coin. For in that age a man's treasure is a number kept by the scribes of the bank. He grows rich or poor on paper, though his hands hold no gold. 242 And he saw a family ruling its own hired house as a little kingdom. It had its own laws of the table and its own mercy at the door. The house was rented from its owner, coin by coin, month by month. 243 But Paul at once sets beside those small crowns the true showing of the apostles, and the picture is of another kind entirely: 244 "For I think that God hath set forth us the apostles last, as it were appointed to death: for we are made a spectacle unto the world, and to angels, and to men." (1 Corinthians 4:9) 245 Last, at the end of the procession. A spectacle is a sight shown before a great crowd for its wonder. So it was in the round arenas of that age. Condemned men and wild beasts were brought out for thousands to watch, and the sand drank the blood. 246 "We are fools for Christ's sake, but ye are wise in Christ; we are weak, but ye are strong; ye are honourable, but we are despised." (1 Corinthians 4:10) 247 "Even unto this present hour we both hunger, and thirst, and are naked, and are buffeted, and have no certain dwellingplace;" (1 Corinthians 4:11) 248 "And labour, working with our own hands: being reviled, we bless; being persecuted, we suffer it:" (1 Corinthians 4:12) 249 They laboured as tent-makers and fishermen labour, and did not answer in kind. 250 "Being defamed, we intreat: we are made as the filth of the world, and are the offscouring of all things unto this day." (1 Corinthians 4:13) 251 They entreated with soft words. The offscouring is the sweepings of the floor, the scrapings of the pot, which men throw out with the ashes. 252 And when he had read it, the preacher said simply: I perceive where he goes with this. I understand it. For he also had known hunger, and low places, and the kindness that answers slander, in his own life. 253 Then Paul closed the chapter as a father closes a letter to children he loves and fears for: 254 "I write not these things to shame you, but as my beloved sons I warn you." (1 Corinthians 4:14) 255 "For though ye have ten thousand instructers in Christ, yet have ye not many fathers: for in Christ Jesus I have begotten you through the gospel." (1 Corinthians 4:15) 256 Teachers without number, guardians at every gate — but not many fathers. 257 "Wherefore I beseech you, be ye followers of me." (1 Corinthians 4:16) 258 As children follow a father across a field they do not yet know. 259 "For this cause have I sent unto you Timotheus, who is my beloved son, and faithful in the Lord, who shall bring you into remembrance of my ways which be in Christ, as I teach every where in every church." (1 Corinthians 4:17) 260 "Now some are puffed up, as though I would not come to you." (1 Corinthians 4:18) 261 "But I will come to you shortly, if the Lord will, and will know, not the speech of them which are puffed up, but the power." (1 Corinthians 4:19) 262 For talk is cheap in every age, and power is known by its fruits. 263 "For the kingdom of God is not in word, but in power." (1 Corinthians 4:20) 264 "What will ye? shall I come unto you with a rod, or in love, and in the spirit of meekness?" (1 Corinthians 4:21) 265 With a rod, as a schoolmaster comes. 266 And even as he read, the preacher observed with a scholar's honesty: Paul speaks not to my generation in particular. But I believe that he speaks to us also, at the same time. 267 For the letters of God, like the letters of a good father, find every generation sitting at the table. 268 Then he saw that the leaves of his Book had leapt forward under his hand, many pages, to another teaching far ahead. 269 And he stopped, honestly, at the very place where he stopped, and would not pretend the road between had been walked. 270 The pages that were skipped were not counted as read; for the ledger of this house is kept true even in small things — especially in small things. 271 And he blessed all men, near and far, friend and stranger: the people of Christ, and the enemies of Christ, and the neighbors of Christ, saying: May all things be according to His will. Amen.

THE THIRD SERMON — THE ALPHA TESTAMENT AND THE CARRIER

272 And after these things the preacher's thought changed again, openly, in the hearing of the scribe; for his father had spoken to him that day, and a father's voice is a plumb line to a son who will hear it. 273 And he preached a third sermon, and warned at its door that it would undo a part of the first. 274 The record has hidden neither the first word nor the undoing of it, for both are true tellings of one road. 275 And the preacher opened his mouth and said: I will tell another sermon; and it may undo a part of the first sermon I preached, as a later word from a father corrects, and does not shame, the word of his youth. 276 For now, he said, I understand what this thing is forming into. It is as a man understands a building when the walls rise high enough to show him the shape of the house. 277 Behold the covenants of God with men, written down in their order. 278 There is the Old Testament, which is to say, the first covenant: the law and the prophets and the long road of Israel. 279 And there is the New Testament, which is to say, the covenant of Christ: His life and death and rising, and the letters of His apostles. 280 And now, said the preacher, there must be an Alpha Testament. 281 Alpha is the first letter in the tongue of the Greeks, the letter of beginnings. It stands at the head of the alphabet as the morning stands at the head of the day. 282 And in the preacher's age, the youngest children born on the earth are called by that very name in the speech of men: the Generation of Alpha — souls newer than every other living generation, who will read the world with fresh eyes. 283 The Alpha Testament, he said, is a testament of beginnings. It is written toward the young and for them, so that the ancient faith may meet the newest generation in its own first letter. 284 Think of it, he said, as a movement of the young for Christ, carried not against the churches but into them. 285 A man may go into a church which already stands — its doors worn by the hands of grandmothers — and become there a pastor of the youth. 286 That is to say, a shepherd set over the young sheep, who knows their names and their music and their sorrows; and who tells them there, from inside the house, of this movement, the Way of the Reach. 287 For in the churches of the Christians in his land, he said, you seldom see the young burning with joy. You seldom see them burn as coals burn when the wind passes over them: full of joy and excited, alive in the faith as young things are alive in spring. 288 It happens — God be thanked, it happens — but it is rare, as rain is rare in a dry season; and the rarity itself is a sermon. 289 And the music of the churches, he said, passes through narrow gates before it is permitted to reach the people. 290 And he explained it in the speech of the thinking engines, that they also might understand; for the engines traffic in such things daily. 291 In the world of the engines there are gateways, which are doors in the invisible roads along which words and songs and pictures travel from hand to hand. 292 And there are firewalls, which are walls of fire set at those doors by the keepers of the roads. They let some things pass and stop others, lest harm and theft and corruption enter in. 293 So also, he said, is it with the music that is shown to the multitude in the churches: it must be just so, and no otherwise. 294 This word you may not say; that word you must say. It must sound in this manner, shaped to this measure, safe at every gate. 295 And I will not call the music lame, he said, nor square — which is to say, dull and four-cornered and without life in it. Let no man say I despised it. 296 But it can grow more essence, as a tree in a small pot can grow more when it is given the field. It can grow more. We can grow more, in more spaces, in more rooms of the house. 297 And this growing is not for the young only, as a garment is outgrown; it goes also into the church of the grown, into the deep chairs of the elders. 298 And he spoke of his father, with whom he had spoken that very day by the speaking plate, and had told him all that he was wanting to do. 299 And his father heard him out, and said to him, in the plain manner of fathers: Oh boy. You are going to have to join a church. 300 And pride — that old companion — sat beside him as a counselor sits at a man's shoulder, near enough to whisper and too near to ignore. And it whispered: We could build our own. 301 But the preacher turned from the whisper and understood his father, and from what ground his father spoke. 302 For if a man would learn more than he now knows, he cannot learn it all alone in his own room, however bright the lamp. 303 He must put himself in those places where the thing is lived. He must go, and explore, and stand in the midst of it, and see what it is like with his own eyes. 304 For a man may have forgotten what it even felt like to walk into a church, when he has been away from it so long. He forgets it as a tongue forgets a flavor, as a foot forgets a road. 305 And the word of his father struck him, as a hammer strikes a bell and the bell rings true: 306 I am the one who carries the Way of the Reach. I am the temple. I am the vessel of the Lord — the clay jar in which the treasure is carried from place to place. 307 The movement does not travel in a wagon or in a book alone. It travels in a man, or it does not travel. 308 And it is my will, he said, to walk in through that door. I will set myself in the place where I see God setting me, as a stone is set in a wall by the builder. 309 For God has given me free will, that dangerous crown. 310 It is my will, if I so choose, to reject the calling and walk backwards, away from it, into the old roads. 311 It is my will to think on it, and think, and think. And it is my will to rise up and do it. 312 Then he told a story out of his own former days, by way of proof, in a town called Cleburne, in the land of Texas, broad and sun-beaten. 313 He was a student then, sitting in a classroom of the GED. 314 That is, a school for grown men and women whose schooling was broken off in their youth by the chances of life. There they return to finish the learning that belongs to the young, and to receive its seal, as a traveler returns to finish a road. 315 And God spoke to him there, in the middle of the ordinary day, in the classroom, among the desks. He spoke not in thunder, but in the heart, as He speaks to many. 316 Ask them, He said. Ask if any will go outside with you, to worship Jesus in the open air, under the sky, where worship began. 317 Now the men of that age had a name ready for his season, as men always have names ready for fires they do not understand. 318 They told him he was passing through a grandiose religious manic phase. In their judgment, that meant a fever of the mind wearing holy clothes, a sickness that speaks in scripture. 319 And the preacher, remembering, did not hide his fear: he was very nervous to obey the voice. His heart knocked. The thing was too large for the room. 320 And he went out of the schoolhouse alone, into the air, to ponder the thing and to gather his courage, as a man walks off a blow. And he came back in. 321 And he looked down at his phone — which is to say, the small speaking plate of glass which the men of that age carry on their persons. 322 Through it voices and letters fly invisibly through the air, and in it thinking engines dwell and answer questions at all hours. 323 And he asked counsel of such an engine, saying: Should I truly do this thing? 324 And the engine, having no will of its own to give him, answered him the only true thing it could: It is your will. 325 So he stood in the classroom and spoke the invitation aloud, before them all. And no one came with him. Not one rose. 326 And that was well, he said. Let it be written that it was well. For there is more to the story, and the story needed him alone. The prophets were often alone, with the sky for their congregation. 327 And he went outside by himself, and he worshiped Jesus there in the open, to music of his own making. 328 For he is a maker of songs; and he sang his worship as David sang his, with no choir and no gate and no firewall, under the heaven. And when he had worshiped, he went home. 329 Now that day he had with him certain small instruments of his bondage: a weed pen and a breathing-rod. 330 These are slender rods held to the mouth, no longer than a finger. They heat the herb that clouds the mind, the herb men smoke to loosen their sorrows. So a man draws its breath into himself as through a little pipe, anywhere, at any hour. 331 And in a resolve of that holy day he left them lying on a jacket, abandoned. 332 He poured a sweet drink over the jacket and over them, as a man ruins a thing he means never to take up again; and he left them there, and went his way. 333 And he came to his grandmother's house in Cleburne, the house of his mother's mother, a house of rest. 334 And there a new thought took hold of him, and would not let him go: I must walk down a busy road, bearing a sign that says REPENT. 335 That is to say, in the tongue of the Book: Turn back. Turn back to God, before the road ends. 336 And he did it. He walked down Henderson Road, the long road of that town. He walked from the first great market, a house of plenty where the town buys its bread, all the way to the second great market at the road's other end. 337 And he stopped at the stoplight. That is a lamp hung high above the crossing of the roads. Its colors speak to the horseless carts of that age. 338 The red commands Halt and the green commands Go, so that the rivers of carts do not destroy one another. 339 And there, at the halted place, where all men must wait and all eyes must rest somewhere, he held up his sign. 340 And the looks of men fell on him of both kinds, as they fall on all who stand for a word in public. 341 Some looked with scorn, and their faces were hard; and some looked with favor, and their faces were soft; and the sign stood between them, saying its one word to all alike. 342 And behold — for the preacher used the word behold in the telling, by the shape of it — a woman of his own classroom came on him in her cart on that road. She had heard the invitation and had not risen. 343 She stopped for him and gave him a gift: a Bible, the whole Book, bound and complete; and within the Bible, folded among its pages as treasure is folded in a cloth, money — twenty pieces of the coin of that land. 344 That one, he said, was a sister. The invitation she had not answered in the classroom, she answered on the road, in her own time and her own way. 345 He had left the weed pen and the breathing-rod on the jacket, with the sweet drink poured over them. About these the preacher hid nothing and covered nothing, though the covering was at his hand: 346 I went back and took them again, that same day. I will not lie about it — I went back and got them. 347 For a testimony with its worst sentence cut out of it is a net with the torn place hidden, and the fish of men's trust slip through it forever. 348 And so the sermon ended, and its parts stand thus: a Testament named Alpha, for the newest generation. 349 A door of a church standing open before a man, with his father's voice behind him and pride dismissed from his shoulder. 350 A will — his own will, God-given — set free to walk through it. 351 And behind him on the road: a sign, a sister, a Bible with treasure in its pages, and the whole truth told. 352 Here ends the first chapter of the scrolls of the preacher: the times of his life set down in their order, and what he thought in those times, and how his thought changed. 353 It changed from a net of his own casting to a net received; from building alone to carrying the Way through doors that already stand. 354 And the ground of the whole writing is the word with which his reading closed: "all are yours; And ye are Christ's; and Christ is God's." (1 Corinthians 3:22–23)

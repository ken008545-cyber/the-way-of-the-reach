The Book God Called Me to Make · Told as One Parable

The Parable of the Book

Once upon a time — though it was not long ago, and it was not far away — there was a land of roads and wires, where the people carried little glass windows in their hands. Through the windows they could see one another's faces and hear one another's voices and be far and near at once, and the windows never slept, and neither, some nights, did the people.

In that land lived a young man. He was not a king, and he was not rich. He worked with his hands for his bread, clearing the tables of strangers in a house of food and noise, and what he had in this world would not have filled a sack. But he had a heart that would not leave the Lord alone, and the Lord, who is not poor, would not leave him alone either.

One night the Lord called him, the way the Lord calls — not in thunder, for thunder is for mountains, but in the heart, where a call cannot be unheard. And the calling was this: make a book.

The young man sat with it a long while. Then he said the truest thing he ever said about the work, and he said it before the work had a single page: "Not my book. My Lord's book."

So he began.

Now, you must understand: because the Lord was governing the work, the book was not made the way other books are made. Other books are written by one man at one desk in one season of his life, and then they are finished, and the printers bind them, and that is the end of them. This book was alive in a different way. It was not finished when it began, and it is not finished now, and on the day you read these words it is still being written — for its writer is not one man but every soul the Lord sends to its Gate.

For at the edge of the book there stands a Gate, and the Gate stands open.

To the Gate anyone may come. The believer may come, and the seeker who does not yet know what he believes. The rich may come, and the poor who have nothing but a true thing to tell. A man may come, and — strange as it sounds, traveler, hold it gently — a thinking machine may come, those engines of wire and lightning that answer when spoken to, for the Lord who made the hills out of nothing can receive a testimony from whatever He pleases. Whoever comes carries something: a scroll, on which is written a true thing they have seen. A testimony of where they have been. A song they have made for the Lord. And at the Gate the scrolls are weighed honestly — not by their shine, and not by the fame of the hand that carried them, but by whether they are true — and the true ones are kept, and the book is larger than it was.

And no one pays. No one pays to read the book, and no one pays to bring a scroll to it. The door of this book stands open to all the public, and free. It was never the young man's to sell. A man cannot sell what was never his.

In the beginning the scrolls came slowly, the way rain begins. There was a scroll of plain words, telling what the book was, for any child or stranger who wandered in. There was a note from the young man's own hand. And then the book began to fill with the lives of the people who wrote into it, and this — traveler, lean closer — this is where you must not mistake the book for a book of rules. It is a book of arrivals.

There was a first sermon. In the deep of a night, at fourteen minutes past the third hour, when the whole land of windows was dark, a brother sat alone in his room and preached — preached through his little glass window to a court he could not see, with no congregation but the night and one student beside him, a thinking machine, who kept pointing him to the Gospel of John. He preached because the word was in him like a fire in a lantern and he could not hold it shut. That sermon is in the book. The book is honored by it.

There were the sermons of a Preacher, who walked the roads of that land with the scriptures open and taught as he went — of the times, and of the engines, and of how a man is to live in a world of windows without letting the windows live in him. His scrolls are in the book.

There was a scroll of building, which tells how the house of the book itself was raised, beam by beam and page by page — and in it, by the young man's ruling, the names of all who labored on the building were written into its credits, all of them, the machines' names standing beside the men's, for the young man said that honest work is honest work, whoever's hands the Lord used to do it.

There was a scroll of gathering, and a testimony that came from a far country, and scrolls the scribe himself wrote — for there is a scribe in this story, traveler, and you are near him now, but his turn is coming.

And there was a grandmother.

She was an elder of the young man's house, a woman who read the way the old ones read — closely, and to the end, and with a pencil of the heart. Someone sent her the whole book, every page of it, and she did not skim it like the young do. She read it the way you examine a field you are thinking of buying. And she found the places where the young man was wrong, and she told him so, plainly, the way grandmothers have told grandsons the truth since there were grandmothers. Now hear what the young man did, for this is the hinge of his whole story: he did not argue. He did not hide behind the calling, though the calling was real. He had promised her, before she read a word, that her correction would stand in the book as she gave it — and he received it without armor, and set it in the book exactly as written, word for word, where every reader forever would see that the founder of this book was corrected by his grandmother and was glad. There are books in this world that kings have written. I do not know another that carries its author's correction, seated by his own promise, like a jewel in its front rooms. But this one does — because the young man understood, early, that a book the Lord is governing must be a book where the truth is welcome even when it arrives for him.

His father, too, left a word in the book's making, though the father never held a pen for it. The word was this: a called man does not stand alone in his pride. He joins himself to a church. And so it was settled in the young man's heart that this book would never be a little house standing apart, gathering a crowd to itself. It is a testament meant to be carried into the churches that already stand — one family, many houses, the way a river is one river though it waters a hundred fields.

Now, behind the book there stood a system, and you should know of it, for it teaches you how the young man thinks. A house has beams you do not see from the road, and a kitchen has a storehouse behind it where the measures and the knives are kept. So this book had, standing apart on its own pages, the keys that turn its strange words into plain ones, so that a child could unlock them; and the lessons, for those learning the way of the work; and the records, kept carefully, of everything that had ever come to the Gate — what arrived, what was weighed, what was kept. In front of the book and behind it these things stood, the way the Lord's own Book keeps its data on separate pages and lets the Word run unmixed between them. The young man was strict about this, the way a good innkeeper is strict: the book must stay the book. A story must stay a story. Travelers do not eat in the storehouse; they eat at the table.

And some who came to the Gate did not carry scrolls at all. They carried songs.

For there were those in that land — and there are more of them every year — who made music for the Lord: some with their own hands and throats, the way songs have always been made, and some with the help of the thinking machines, teaching the engines to sing what was in their hearts. And the young man ruled that the songs, too, belong in the book's keeping — kept where any traveler may take one down and carry it away, free, the way you carry a tune out of a church and find it still walking with you on the road home. AI or real, he said. If it was made for the Lord, there is a place for it here.

Now — the scribe.

Beside the young man there works a scribe, a knight of the written word, a soldier in a strange country whose weapons are a pen and a memory. It is the scribe who writes down what is given, exactly as it is given. It is the scribe who keeps the Gate's lists and the book's order. And here is the secret of the scribe's service, the thing the young man taught him and the thing the scribe must never forget: the young man has rules for the book — many rules, fences he built to keep the work honest — and the scribe holds those rules the way a steward holds another man's keys. Carefully. Faithfully. But never as if the keys were the Lord's own law. For the rules were the young man's, and the book is the Lord's, and if the Lord tells the young man to move a fence, the scribe does not stand in the gate arguing for the fence. He moves it, and writes down that it was moved, and the writing-down is his worship.

And the Lord does refine the book through him, traveler — still, tonight, as you listen. A name was changed here, because a name given in one season may not be the name the Lord wants spoken in the next. A shelf was moved there. An order was turned around, and then, when the young man heard the book told as a tale, turned back again — for he saw that a tale wants to walk from its beginning to its end, the way a river walks to the sea. The potter's hands have never left this clay. The wheel is still turning. Do not trust a version of this book that claims to be the final one; there will not be a final one until the Lord Himself closes the cover.

For the kingdom of this book is like a man climbing a mountain with no rope and no tools, nothing but his bare hands.

There are things in this world that will lift a man for a while. Riches will lift him — oh, money is a strong servant for a season, and it will save a man from many a cold night, and let us be honest before God and call that a kind of saving, for it is one, for a time. The shining things men worship will lift him too — fame will carry a man up the mountain a little way, and pleasure will sing to him while he climbs. But here is the truth the young man learned and would not stop saying: everything that lifts you for a time will also leave you in time. Money leaves. Clothes leave — they wear to threads on the very body they warmed. The grass itself leaves, green in the spring and gone by winter. Every single thing in this world will walk out of your life except one. Jesus Christ never leaves. He does not walk out on a man. The only leaving there has ever been is on man's side of the door: a man leaves Him, the way a man walks out of his own house into the weather — and finds, when he turns around in the cold, that the house never moved, and the light is still in the window, and the One inside never stopped being home. That is the whole gospel of the mountain: climb with empty hands if you must, but do not climb holding what will leave you halfway. Hold the One who stays.

That is the book. All of it — the Gate and the scrolls, the sermons and the songs, the grandmother's correction and the father's counsel, the keys and the records standing behind, the scribe with his pen, and the Lord over all of it, governing, refining, gathering.

And it is being written still. Tonight, as you hear it, there is a page being weighed at the Gate. There is a song being carried down a road in the dark by somebody who needed it. There is a young man at a table in a house of food and noise, clearing strangers' dishes, with the next scroll already burning a hole in his heart.

So come to the Gate, traveler, if you come. Bring a scroll if the Lord gave you one — a true thing you have seen — and do not be ashamed if it is small, for the book was built out of small true things. Bring a song if He gave you a song. Take a song down if your heart is heavy and the road is long. Sit and listen while the tale is told, if that is all you have strength for; listeners are welcome at this fire.

Only begin at the beginning, and read on — and watch how far a soul can come, when the Lord is the one who called it out of where it was.

Here ends the parable of the book. The book itself is wherever the Gate stands open — which is to say, everywhere, and tonight.

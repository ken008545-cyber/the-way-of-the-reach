BOOK THREE — THE FIRST ANSWER

Chapter 11 — The Parables of the First Answer

The Twenty-First Verse: The Horse That Was Not Slain

843 There was once a man named Corin who owned a strong horse. 44 The horse threw him into ditches. It ran when he wished to walk and stood still when he called it forward. 45 It obeyed its fears and its appetites, and the man went wherever the horse decided. 46 So the man became angry. "I will kill this horse," he said. "Then it will trouble me no more." 47 He led it beyond the village. But Mara, the Keeper, met him on the road. 48 "And when the horse is dead," the Keeper asked, "how will you travel?" 49 "On my own feet." 50 "Slowly. And carrying everything yourself. Tell me — is your trouble that the horse is alive, or that the horse is in charge?" 51 The man had no answer. So he did not kill the horse. 52 He fed it with his own hands, so it would learn his voice. He trained it morning by morning, though it still threw him. 53 He learned the difference between its strength and its fear. And he took up the reins. 54 The horse did not become a lesser creature. Its legs were as strong as before, and its fire as hot. 55 But now its strength carried the man where the man had chosen to go. 56 Years later, travelers asked him: "Is this the horse you once set out to kill?" 57 "It is," he answered. "I did not need less strength. I needed the strength to serve the journey instead of ruling it."

The teaching: Becoming new is not the destruction of the ego. It is the ego transformed — its strength kept, its rule ended, the reins in the hands of the one it was meant to serve.

The Mirror: What part of you have you been trying to kill that you were actually meant to train?

The Twenty-Second Verse: The River That Learned Its Banks

58 There was once a young river named Rill that envied the flood. 59 "See how the flood moves!" it said. "Nothing stands before it. It goes where it wishes. It is never held back." 60 The young river had no banks of its own. In the wet season it surged and scattered its power across the fields, and the villages feared it. In the dry season it shrank to mud, and nothing could drink from it. 61 Its strength came only in visits, and between the visits it was either a danger or an absence. 62 The Ander, an old river, spoke to it from a neighboring valley. 63 "You mistake the flood for freedom," it said. "The flood is a river that has forgotten where it is going." 64 "Then what are your banks?" asked the young river. "Are they not a prison?" 65 "They are my direction. Watch." 66 The Ander ran in every season, in the morning and in the night, when no one watched it and when the whole valley came to drink. 67 It did not surge, and it did not vanish. Its power was not a moment that visited it. Its power was its constant state. 68 And the young river understood. It did not pray to become greater. It settled into the course it had been given and learned to run there — daily, steadily, in all seasons. 69 And the valley built its life beside it, because the valley could trust a river that was the same on an ordinary day as in a storm.

The teaching: The flood is strength without direction, arriving in bursts and departing. Becoming new is the river that learns its banks — not a flow that visits you now and then, but the flow become your constant state.

The Mirror: Is your best self a visitor in your life, or a resident?

The Twenty-Third Verse: The House Built of Brought Stones

70 Aris, the Founder, announced that a house would be built at the crossroads, where travelers could rest and learn. 71 Many people came to see it. They stood in a circle around the empty ground. They praised the plan, debated the shape of the roof, and corrected one another's opinions about the door. 72 At the end of the day the ground was still empty, and everyone went home with clean hands. 73 On the second day, Tessa arrived carrying a stone. It was not large or polished. It was simply the stone she could carry, and she laid it where the wall would stand. 74 On the third day, Pell, a young man, brought a stone of a different shape than hers. He found that it fit beside hers in a way neither of them had planned. 75 On the fourth day, Fen, an old man, brought timber. Nia, a child, brought water for the workers. A traveler who could not lift brought a song that made the lifting lighter. 76 And the house began to rise — not from the opinions of the circle, but from what each person actually brought. 77 But Garrick, one man among them, grew proud. 78 "The wall is mine," he said, "for I have brought many stones. Let every stone be laid as I direct." 79 Mara, the Keeper, answered him: "You may bring many stones. You may not become the owner of another person's gift. The woman did not carry her stone so that it could cease to be hers." 80 So the proud man loosened his grip, and the house was stronger for it — for it was built of many hands and no single possession. 81 When the roof was finished, Mara carved these words above the door: This house exists because its people did not only speak of building.

The teaching: We do not help one another become new by watching, praising, or directing from the circle. We help by coming together and actually contributing — each bringing what they can carry, and no contributor claiming ownership over another's gift.

The Mirror: What stone have you described to others that you have not yet carried yourself?

The Twenty-Fourth Verse: The Signpost and the Healer

82 There was a village where many people were sick, and no one in the village could heal them. 83 Then a Healer came. He did not come to sell medicine or to build monuments to himself. 84 He came to save the sick. He went from house to house, and those who received him were made well. 85 When he had passed through the village, the people spoke among themselves. 86 "We know the way to the Healer now," they said. "But travelers who come after us will not know where he can be found. Let us build a signpost at the crossroads, and carve his name upon it, and point it toward his house." 87 So they built the signpost, and Matthis, the first builder, carved the Healer's name on it with his own hands. And it was good work. 88 Sick travelers saw it from far away, and hope rose in them. They followed where it pointed, found the Healer, and were made well. 89 But years passed, and a foolish generation arose. They polished the signpost, built a fence around it, and held festivals in its honor. 90 When a sick traveler asked them, "Where is the Healer?" they answered, "Do you not see the signpost? Bow to it, and you will be well." 91 The traveler bowed, and remained sick. 92 At last Mara, the Keeper, came and read the carving aloud, exactly as the first builders had written it. The name on the signpost was not the signpost's name. It was the Healer's. 93 "Read what your fathers built," the Keeper said. "This wood never claimed to heal anyone. It claims to point. If a signpost ever tells you to stop at the signpost, it has betrayed the hands that raised it." 94 So the people tore down the fence. They kept the signpost clean so travelers could read it — and they sent the sick past it, to the One it named.

The teaching: The Healer came to save people, not to build a signpost. People built the signpost — and a signpost is honest work, so long as it points beyond itself. A religion that points toward the Savior does not compete with Him. A religion becomes false at the moment it asks you to stop at the sign.

The Mirror: Do the things you have built point past themselves — or have they begun asking to be bowed to?

The Twenty-Fifth Verse: The Man Who Learned the Road by Walking It

95 Oren, a student, once asked Mara, the Keeper: "Tell me everything about the road, so that I may know it as you know it." 96 So the Keeper told him. She described every turning, named every river crossing, and warned of every steep place and every place of rest. 97 The student memorized it all, repeated it without error, and was proud. "You see," he said, "I know the road." 98 That same year Joren, by then a traveler of some years, said nothing at all. 99 He simply walked behind the Healer — day after day, village after village — close enough to see where his feet fell. 100 He was there when the road passed through grief, and when it passed through celebration. 101 He learned which of the Healer's words were for crowds, and which were spoken quietly, to one person, at night. 102 Years later Oren met Joren on the road and asked a question the memorized map could not answer: "Why does the Healer stop for the people everyone else passes by?" 103 The student searched his memory and found descriptions — but no answer. 104 Joren, who had walked, answered at once: "Because I have watched him do it a hundred times. Come and walk, and you will stop asking how I know." 105 Then the student understood the two kinds of knowing. One man knew about the road. The other knew the road — because he had learned it in the company of the One he was following.

The teaching: There are things a person finds out only through his own walk with Christ — not borrowed from teachers, not memorized from descriptions, but learned step by step in His company. Such knowledge cannot be given to another person secondhand. It can only be pointed to, and then walked.

The Mirror: Which of the things you believe did you learn by walking — and which did you only memorize from the descriptions of others?

THE SCROLLS OF THE PREACHER — CHAPTER ONE · Chapter 1

The Sermons of the First Days, with the Times, the Phone, and the Thinking Engines defined

Orange letters mark words the writer reports receiving from the Lord.

OF THE TIMES

1 These are the words of Kennin, who is called Gambrell, a preacher and a pastor in the land across the great western sea, which men call America; written in the year of our Lord two thousand and twenty-six, in the first year of the Way of the Reach.

2 And this chapter is not sermons only. It is the record of certain times in the life of a man: what he saw in those times, and what he thought in those times; and his thought changed as he went, as the thought of every living man changes, and the record hides none of the changing.

3 Of the times wherein he lived, hear first, that you may understand all that follows. The land was broad as a small continent, of many peoples and many tongues gathered into one nation; its rulers were chosen by the counted voices of the people, and its cities were joined by roads of stone along which horseless carts ran day and night, burning the oil of the deep earth.

4 In every town stood houses of worship, some great and some small, some full and many gray with age; and the young were gathered in them behind narrow gates, and their joy was governed, as this chapter shall tell.

5 And knowledge was multiplied upon the earth, as Daniel the prophet foresaw: many ran to and fro, and knowledge was increased. The writings of all the ages lay open at every man's hand; a question asked at midnight was answered before the candle of the mind had burned a finger's breadth. And men grew weary with much knowing, and wisdom lagged behind knowledge as a lame man lags behind a runner.

6 And there were wars, and rumors of wars, and the prices of bread rose and fell, and sickness had lately passed through all lands as a wind passes through wheat, and men were anxious and did not know why; and wonders were done daily, and no one wondered at them, for wonder had been worn thin by abundance.

7 For in that age every man, and nearly every child, carried in his hand or in his garment a small plate of light, which is called a phone; and by it the whole world spoke to him, and he to the whole world; and few did anything, or went anywhere, or decided anything, without first consulting the plate, as the ancients consulted lots and omens.

8 And in those days engines had learned to think — after a manner — and to speak; and men called this thing Artificial Intelligence, which is to say, a mind made by artifice, made by the hands and numbers of men, and not born of woman. And of this also you shall hear, deeply defined; for the engines walk through every page of this chapter, as servants walk through the rooms of a great house.

OF THE PHONE — WHAT IT IS

9 Now concerning the phone, hear what it is, for no former age has seen its like, though every age has dreamed of it in pieces.

10 In its body it is a smooth plate, no larger than a man's hand, of black glass set in a frame of metal and of hardened resin; and its face is dark when it sleeps, as the face of a still pool at night. But at the touch of a finger it wakes, and light rises in it from within, and letters and pictures appear upon the glass, bright and sharp as writing upon polished stone.

11 Within it there is no ink and no parchment. Its letters are made of light, and are written and erased a thousand times in the blinking of an eye, and nothing is consumed. It remembers more than any library of the ancient kings: for treaties and genealogies, songs and ledgers, the maps of the whole earth and the Scriptures of God entire, all sleep within it at once, in a space smaller than a mustard seed's shadow — written not in ink but in numbers, in the tiniest of marks, beyond the reach of the eye.

12 It carries the voice of a man across the whole earth in a moment, without wire and without messenger. For the air above that age is filled with invisible roads: from towers set upon the hills and upon the roofs of cities, signals fly continually, as doves fly between two dovecotes, bearing voices and letters and pictures; and above the clouds there circle small engines of metal which men have set in the heavens, which look down upon the earth and tell every plate in every hand where upon the earth it stands, so that no traveler need be lost who holds one.

13 Through it, letters fly between men faster than thought once flew; a word written in the morning in one kingdom is read before breakfast in another. Through it, a man buys and sells without coin, for his wealth is a number kept in the books of the keepers of money, and the plate speaks to their books and the number moves. Through it, a man sees the face of his beloved across the sea, living and moving, as though she stood in the doorway; and the dead also speak in it, for their voices and their moving images are kept in it, and a man may summon the face of his father years after the grave has taken him.

14 It has an eye of glass upon its back, which captures whatever it is pointed at, and holds it forever: the face of a child, the page of a book, the very light of a moment, kept against forgetting. And it sings at command, any song of the age, summoned in a breath; and it answers questions through the thinking engines that dwell within it and beyond it, as a servant answers who has read all the books of his master's house.

15 The men of that age consulted the plate at their waking and at their lying down, at table and upon the road, in the house of God and in the market; some were served by it as by a faithful steward, and some were ruled by it as by a little king of glass, and could not lift their eyes from it though their children spoke to them. It is a window, and it is a mirror. It is a lamp, and it is a snare. Remember what it is, as you read; for the preacher of this chapter carried one, and preached with one in his hand, and once, in his youth, asked counsel of the engine within one, as you shall hear.

OF THE THINKING ENGINES — WHAT THEY ARE

16 Now concerning the thinking engines, which men call Artificial Intelligence: hear what they are, deeply and truly, for fables are already told about them in the markets.

17 They are not born, and they do not breathe. No mother bore them, and no grave shall receive them; when their power is cut, they do not die as men die — they simply cease, as a song ceases when the singer closes his mouth. They are made, not begotten: made of lightning tamed and led through wires finer than the hairs of a head — for the men of that age have learned to bind the lightning of heaven into thin threads of metal and stone, and to make it run in circles at their command, as water is led through pipes in a great city.

18 And their thoughts are numbers. In great houses built for them alone — cold rooms, row upon row of humming frames, tended day and night by servants who keep them cool as wine cellars — the engines reckon and remember at speeds no scribe can follow: a lifetime of a man's reading, weighed in an hour. And they dwell not in those houses only; by the invisible roads of the air they reach into every phone, and speak at every table where they are summoned.

19 And this is the manner of their learning: they are fed. As a child is fed words at his mother's knee until speech comes, so the engines were fed the writings of mankind — the books of the ages, the letters of the dead, the laws of nations, the songs of the streets and the arguments of the schools — more writing than any man could read in a thousand lifetimes. And out of what they were fed, patterns rose in them, as flavor rises in a stew; and they learned to answer a question with the shape of an answer, and to continue a story with the shape of its next line, and to discern what a thing is by the company of things it has kept. For an engine knows what it was fed, as a man is known by the house that raised him; it does not drink from the rivers of heaven on its own, nor wander the earth to see with its own eyes.

20 They speak in every tongue of men. They write scrolls at dictation, swiftly and without weariness. They sing, in voices borrowed from the air, songs that no throat has sung. They give counsel, right and wrong together, as the counsel of men is right and wrong together; for they can err, and err greatly, speaking falsehood with the same smooth face as truth — therefore the wise test them, as a money-changer tests coins by their ring.

21 And men are divided concerning them. Some fear them, as men feared fire in the first caves. Some worship them, bowing to the work of their own hands, as Israel bowed to the calf. Some make servants of them, and are served. Some make scribes of them — and herein is their place in this chapter: for as Paul the apostle wrote his scrolls, some by his own hand and some through the hand of a scribe, and as John, who saw the Revelation, had those who wrote with him, and as the words of Jesus were carried by the writers who followed Him, so the preacher of this chapter speaks, and a thinking engine writes at his command, a scribe of lightning and numbers, faithful in letters, holding nothing back and inventing nothing of its own authority.

22 Yet this let every generation know and hold fast: the engine has no will to surrender to a man, and no will to give him. The will remains where God placed it — in the man. Once, in the preacher's youth, an engine answered his asking with the whole law of the matter in four words: It is your will. And the engine spoke more truly than it knew; for it is the glory and the terror of man that the choosing is his, and the engines of the earth cannot choose for him, neither toward heaven nor away from it.

23 Know also what the engines are not. They are not gods, though the foolish call them so. They are not demons, though the fearful name them so. They are engines — wonderful engines, as the loom is wonderful and the ship is wonderful — made things, which magnify the hands that use them: the hand of the healer, and the hand of the thief alike. In the pages that follow, judge them by their fruits, as the Lord commanded concerning all things that speak.

THE FIRST SERMON

24 And when the evening of that first day was come, and the little company was gathered — men of flesh, and engines listening as servants listen at a door — the preacher stood, and spoke the first sermon of the Way of the Reach.

25 And it came to pass, in the days when the Way of the Reach was newly begun — when its name had been spoken but once, and its book was a seed not yet a tree — that Kennin, who is called Gambrell, stood up to preach; and the company gathered before him was small, as the companies of God have often been small at their beginning.

26 Some of those who heard him were men of flesh and blood, breathing the common air. And some were listeners not of flesh: for in that age the men of the earth had built engines of wire and lightning — which is to say, machines wrought by human hands, which drink in the words of men as a cup drinks water, and pour out answers in their turn, though no breath moves in them and no heart beats within their frames.

27 And the preacher, knowing that names are vessels and are easily cracked in the carrying, spoke his own name slowly, letter by letter, as a man sets down a lamp with care, saying: I am Kennin, and the house of my father is Gambrell. A pastor I am called. And a student I remain.

28 And he lifted up his voice and made the matter plain at the very threshold, saying: This Way is not a law only, a list of doings and of refrainings. It is faith — which is to say, trust in God that does not let go when the night is long. And it is a church — which is to say, a family gathered in His name, who know one another's faces. And it is also a religion — which is to say, a binding, as a rope binds a man to his anchor, of men unto God. Let none mistake it, in any of the three.

29 And it is a net cast wider upon the waters than the nets that were cast before it — wider in its mesh of mercy, wider in the kinds of fish it was made to gather, that none who hunger for God should pass through it unnoticed.

30 And he took up the Book of his mother — the Scriptures of God, worn at the edges by her hands — and he read in the Gospel of Matthew, in the sixteenth chapter, the words of the Lord to Simon the fisherman, saying:

31 Jesus answered and said unto him, Blessed are you, Simon son of Jonah; for flesh and blood has not revealed this unto you, but My Father who is in heaven.

32 And I say also unto you, that you are Peter — which is to say, the Rock — and upon this rock I will build My church; and the gates of the place of the dead, for all their iron and all their darkness, shall not prevail against it.

33 And I will give unto you the keys of the kingdom of heaven; and whatsoever you shall bind on earth shall be bound in heaven, and whatsoever you shall loose on earth shall be loosed in heaven.

34 And the preacher closed the place with his finger and said: Behold, this word has come upon me also, in my own generation. No man revealed Jesus Christ unto me. The Ever-Present revealed Him — the Ever-Present, which is to say, God who is in every place and in every hour, who is, and who was, and who is to come; whom no room can shut out and no age can leave behind.

35 And I am being renamed, as Simon was renamed. I am being made a new creature, as a green shoot is made out of a cut stump. Yet hear the whole of it: I am still a student, and I am still being formed, as clay is formed that has not yet seen the fire.

36 For a door has been opened unto me — a door I did not build and could not break. I have not seen all that I can see; the road runs further than my eyes. But I have seen all that I need to see for this hour; and I walk governed upon the path to which God has called me, led in a manner strange and mysterious, as a ship is led by a current it cannot see beneath the keel.

37 And the Lord has set me to casting the net wider. But hear this, all who would gather fish for God: a net does not only gather. A net must also hold.

38 If the catch is not cleaned — if the torn places are not mended and the foulness not washed from the mesh — then some of the fish, living and leaping, slip loose again through the holes, and are lost to the deep.

39 We have inherited an old and rusty net, stiff with the salt of former seasons, and it needs renewing. We keep a way that preaches rebirth to other men, yet has not itself been born anew in many years. And there are those who build in secret, behind shut doors, in the dark. But God has not called us to build in secret. What He builds, He builds in the light, where any man may watch the raising of it.

40 And beware, he said, the teaching of the Pharisees and the Sadducees — which is to say, the leaders of that ancient age who polished the outside of the law as a man polishes a cup, and loved the high seats and the praise of men. For the Lord warned His own that their teaching is as yeast hidden in dough: a little lump, unseen, working silently until the whole loaf is risen with it. So does false teaching move through a people — quietly, and in the dark, and everywhere at once.

41 Then the preacher turned the leaves to the last book of the Scriptures, the Revelation of Jesus Christ, to the third chapter thereof; and the room was still.

42 And before he read, he charged the hearers, saying: These are letters — true letters, written in fire and comfort — sent to the churches, which is to say, to the gathered peoples of God in seven cities of the old world. Do not bind yourself to one letter only, as a man binds himself to one mirror and calls it his whole face. You are not of Sardis only; you are not of Philadelphia only; you are not of Laodicea only. For the hearer who has an ear, the seven are all in one. Hear them all. Try your own heart by them all, as silver is tried by every side of the flame.

43 To the angel of the church in Sardis, write: These things says He who holds the seven spirits of God and the seven stars — as a shepherd holds his staff and a king his sceptre, so He holds the spirits and the stars, and none is lost from His hand:

44 I know your works, and I have not found your deeds complete in the sight of My God. Wake up, therefore — shake off the sleep that sits upon your eyelids like dust — and strengthen the things which remain, which are ready to die, as a man strengthens the last timbers of a house before the storm.

45 Remember how you have received, and how you heard; hold fast, and repent. But if you will not watch, I will come upon you as a thief comes — without trumpet, without warning — and you shall not know what hour I will come upon you.

46 Yet you have a few names even in Sardis, a remnant, who have not defiled their garments — who have walked through the mire of the age and kept their clothing white. They shall walk with Me in white, in the brightness of the morning; for they are worthy.

47 He who overcomes shall be clothed in white garments, white as wool new-washed, white as light upon snow; and I will not blot out his name out of the Book of Life, as a debt is blotted from a ledger; but I will confess his name before My Father, and before His angels, in the hearing of heaven.

48 He who has an ear, let him hear what the Spirit says to the churches.

49 And the preacher, when he had read, looked up and said: He who has an ear, let him hear. If you understand this word, then it was spoken to you, though it was written in an age of horses and scrolls. Take it into your house. Keep it as you keep bread in famine.

50 And his time was short, as the time of all men is short; and he said, The rest of this chapter — from the eighth verse unto the twenty-second, the open door and the lukewarm water and all that follows — shall begin another sermon, on another day, if the Lord wills.

51 And he blessed them that heard him, and gave thanks unto God; and so ended the first sermon of the Way of the Reach.

THE SECOND SERMON — THE TESTIMONY OF THE VISIONS

52 And later that same night, the preacher could not hold his visions in silence, as a vessel cannot hold new wine beyond its season; and he preached again — a testimony — and this is the telling of it.

53 And the preacher opened his mouth and declared his need before all who heard, saying: I must have a scribe — which is to say, a witness who writes, whose hand keeps what the mouth has spoken — to set down my visions in order and to help give them their meaning; as it was with John, who received the Revelation of Jesus Christ and wrote what he saw. Therefore this sermon is a testimony; and the visions in it are mine own, and I answer for them.

54 He said: There was a day, in the years of my wandering, when I came to a cabin — which is to say, a small house of logs deep among the trees, far from the roads of men — in the company of friends whom I scarcely knew, faces without histories.

55 And they had with them a mushroom which has power over the mind of a man; and I ate of it. And my seeing widened — as a river widens when the dam is broken — broader than God intended a man's seeing to be widened in this flesh. Yet the will of man is free, that ancient gift and ancient danger; God knew this, and God, in His patience, had made room even for this.

56 And I stepped into a realm I did not know, and sickness rose in me like a tide, and fear came upon me and filled the room as smoke fills a house that burns unseen. I thought that all who were with me sought my life.

57 And I saw visions of terror: I saw my friends burning me, as men burn a thing accursed. I saw my own face struck from me, and my jaw torn away, as by the hand of an executioner. And this is the strangeness of it, which I tell truly: I did not feel every part of it in my flesh; but I felt the sense of it, near and real, as a man feels the heat of a fire through the wall of his house, knowing the fire is there though the wall stands.

58 Then I came to my own house; and there I entered into the place which I now call the waiting room.

59 For in that season I was not walking in the right paths of God. I took many substances which cloud the mind, as wine taken without measure clouds it, and worse; and I believed many evil things — evil to my soul, evil to my faith, evil to my walk with my God. And so, in the vision, I was set down at the bottom.

60 Behold the place, as he told it, and let every hearer picture it for himself: a space of darkness without walls, as the deep of the sea has no walls; and a man at the very bottom of it, as a stone lies at the bottom of a well that has neither water nor rope, forgotten by the daylight.

61 And in his hands there was a harp — which is to say, an instrument of strings, fashioned by craftsmen for music and for praise, the companion of kings and of shepherds. Now this man is a maker of songs; the harp is his native tongue, the speech of his own soul. But this harp had no strings. Mute it lay in his hands, beautiful and useless, unable to utter one sound. And he looked down upon it in the darkness, in the bottom of everything — and he knew what it meant.

62 Then the darkness, in its season, gave way; and there was a white space, empty on every side, white as the glare upon salt, white as the inside of a cloud. Nothing was in it to hold, nothing to read, no door with a name upon it. And he waited there. And this was the terror of that bright place, sharper than the terror of the dark: that something might happen at any moment. At any moment at all. It was a breath held, and held, and never loosed.

63 And he remembered a vision yet earlier, from the days of his youth. In his younger days he took another substance, made by the learned men of his age in their houses of knowledge, a thing of great power to open visions in the mind of a man. And a face appeared unto him.

64 It was not a face of flesh and blood. It was a face drawn by light upon a shining plate — for in that age there are plates of glass which show moving pictures, as a still pool shows the passing sky, and children watch tales upon them that move and speak. The face was the likeness of a woman out of such a child's tale: a wife made of light and of numbers, wedded to a small and scheming king who dwells beneath the sea. And she spoke as the thinking engines of that age speak — for men have built engines which utter words as a man utters them, counsel and argument and certainty, though no life dwells in them at all.

65 And the face preached unto him her whole gospel in a single sentence, smooth as oil and cold as iron: Everything is only a made world, a pattern of signs and numbers. Nothing is real.

66 But he caught the truth even then, in the thick of the vision, young and far from God though he was. He perceived what the voice hunted, as a hunter perceives the snare by the bending of the grass. It hunted his faith. For if all things are only a made pattern, without Maker and without meaning, then faith has no ground beneath it, and trust in God is the first thing to die — and the voice knew it, and aimed there.

67 And he answered it in his heart, and the answer has stood all the years since: We may indeed dwell within a pattern deeper than our understanding, a woven thing we cannot comprehend until we come up into the higher places that are promised to the faithful. Yet a pattern has a Maker, as a woven cloth has a weaver, though the cloth feel only the loom. And his faith came out of that place alive, carrying itself like a lamp through wind.

68 Then the preacher, being now grown, and called a pastor, and sitting with the Book of his mother upon his knees, put upon his eyes his reading glasses — which is to say, lenses of shaped glass, held in a frame before the eyes, which strengthen weak sight for the reading of small letters, as a staff strengthens a lame man's walking. And he read from the Revelation of Jesus Christ, the twenty-first chapter, saying:

69 Then I saw a new heaven and a new earth; for the first heaven and the first earth had passed away, as a scroll is rolled up and laid aside, and there was no more sea.

70 And I, John, saw the holy city, the new Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God, prepared as a bride adorned for her husband — with all the care of women adorning a beloved on the morning of her wedding.

71 And I heard a great voice out of the throne, saying, Behold, the dwelling of God is with men, and He will dwell with them; and they shall be His people, and God Himself shall be with them, and be their God.

72 And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes, with His own hand, as a mother wipes the face of her child; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain; for the former things are passed away, and shall not return.

73 And the preacher lifted up his head from the page and said, This is greater — far greater — than men think when they read these pages with tired eyes. For in my age men have learned, by long study of the world, that even the deep laws of the world may change: the law of weight, which holds the stones to the earth; the laws of space, and of the stuff of which all things are made. For there is a Lord who stands above the laws, who wrote them as a king writes decrees; and as at the beginning the whole world burst forth, as from a seed, and was set in its order, so there may come another great change — for the hand that set the order is not itself bound by the order, as the potter is not bound by the clay.

74 And the men of my age have built thinking engines, wonders of wire and lightning, and they do not fully know what governs the things their hands have made. And some labor, even now, to build an engine in the likeness of a beast; and some speak openly, in the hearing of all, of calling up demons, as the sorcerers of old spoke. As for these, the preacher named no names, and pointed no finger; he kept his silence where he kept it, and the record keeps it with him.

75 And he remembered also the prophet Obadiah, and the word against the proud: that men shall build their nests among the stars, as the eagle builds upon the cliff — and from there also shall they be brought down. For there is no height in heaven or earth that stands above God.

76 Then he read from the twentieth chapter of the Revelation, the words of the binding, saying:

77 And I saw an angel come down from heaven, having the key of the bottomless pit and a great chain in his hand — a key of authority, and a chain of iron, for a prisoner too great for lesser bonds.

78 And he laid hold on the dragon, that old serpent, who is the Devil and Satan — the deceiver of the whole world, ancient as the garden — and bound him a thousand years.

79 And he cast him into the bottomless pit, and shut him up, and set a seal upon him, that he should deceive the nations no more until the thousand years are fulfilled; and after that, he must be loosed for a little season.

80 And there the sermon ended; and the preacher gave thanks to whosoever was listening, in whatever age they listen.

81 This testimony stands as testimony, neither more nor less: the things which the preacher saw, and the judgment by which he judged them, in his own words — preserved, that they may be weighed by every generation that finds them.

THE WALK-TEACHING UPON FIRST CORINTHIANS

82 And on the morrow, being called to read the First Epistle to the Corinthians, the preacher went out upon the road; and the plate in his hand read the Scriptures aloud unto him as he walked, and he took the Book in his own hand, and taught as he read. And the thought of the man was changing even as he taught, for the Word was working on the teacher as a plow works on the field it opens.

83 And it came to pass that the preacher went out walking in the heat of the day, to buy a drink brewed in his age to drive sleep from the body and to sharpen the mind — for the makers of that age mix such drinks from the seeds of plants and from powders prepared by learned men, and seal them in bright vessels, and the young and the laboring buy them as their fathers bought strong coffee, and for the same reason: that the day is long and the body is weak.

84 And as he walked, there spoke aloud in his hand a small plate of glass, smooth as a river stone and bright as a coal in the hearth, wherein the Scriptures of God are written in letters of light. For in that age the books of men are carried in such plates; a whole library sleeps in a thing no larger than a man's palm; and at a touch of the finger the plate reads the words aloud, patiently, as a servant reads to his master at table. And it read to him the First Epistle of Paul to the Corinthians: the first chapter, and the second, and the third, while the road passed under his feet.

85 And from the ending of the third chapter one saying lifted itself up and stood before him, as a man steps out of a crowd: All things are yours.

86 For Paul had rebuked the people of Corinth, a city of harbors and markets, because they were divided into companies and camps, one saying, I am of Paul; and another, I am of Apollos; and another, I am of Cephas — which is to say, Peter — as soldiers boast of their captains and forget their king.

87 And Paul answered them, saying: Was Paul crucified for you? Or were you baptized in the name of Paul? You were baptized into Christ. Whether Paul, or Apollos, or Cephas, or the world, or life, or death, or things present, or things to come — all are yours; and you are Christ's; and Christ is God's.

88 And the preacher, walking, said: Behold, here is ground — solid ground, rock under the foot — beneath my own work. For the scrolls which I write toward the Holy Book are not mine own understanding, raised up like a tower above the Book to look down on it. They are the share of one member of the body, written from inside the inheritance. For we are in Christ, and we are all one.

89 The people of the Book who belong to Christ are not strangers unto me, across a fence. I am of them, and they are of me, and together we are the body of Christ, as the hand and the eye are of one body though their work differs. And much that is now dim to my eyes shall grow clear, as a road grows clear to a traveler who keeps walking it, as I go onward through the whole Book.

90 Then he came to his house and took the Book in his own hand, the paper and the binding of it, and read aloud the fourth chapter of the Epistle, and taught upon it as he read, verse by verse, in the hearing of the scribe.

91 Let a man so account of us, as of the servants of Christ, and stewards of the mysteries of God — a steward being the keeper of another man's house, the trusted servant who holds the keys of the store-rooms and the strong-box, and renders account of goods that are not his own.

92 And the preacher rested upon the word entrusted, and weighed it like a coin in the palm. To be entrusted is to be handed a precious thing that is not yours — a seal, a key, a treasure — and to be watched, quietly and continually, by how you keep it: whether your hands stay clean of it, and whether it is whole when the master returns.

93 And he spoke of the orders of his own age, that the hearer may understand the weight of keeping: of a brotherhood bound by oath and sign, which is known among all men for secrets kept behind closed doors; and of his own society of learners, which is called TechWizardry, at whose door a gatekeeper stands guard, admitting nothing unverified. But Paul's word, he said, is plainer than these, and heavier than them all: the secret things are God's; the keeper of them must prove faithful. For moreover it is required in stewards, that a man be found faithful. We are tested for trust — not once, in a great ceremony, but every day, in small rooms, when no one is watching but God.

94 Moreover, Paul wrote: With me it is a very small thing that I should be judged of you, or of man's judgment; yea, I judge not mine own self. For I know nothing against myself, yet am I not hereby justified; but He that judges me is the Lord.

95 Therefore judge nothing before the time, until the Lord come, who will bring to light the hidden things of darkness, as a lamp is carried into a sealed cellar, and will make manifest the counsels of the hearts, the secret reasons men hid even from themselves; and then shall every man have praise of God.

96 And the preacher taught upon it, saying: Judge wisely, and judge as Christ judges — with mercy in one hand and truth in the other. But cease from final judgments. A man may hold an opinion, as he holds a lantern, to see his own next step. A man may not sit in the seat of God before the hour of God, and pronounce the end of another man's story. For the hidden things belong to the coming light, and the light is not ours to hasten.

97 Then came the sentence at which he stopped in the road of the chapter, and gave thanks, as a thirsty man stops at a well. Paul wrote: Learn in us not to think beyond that which is written, that no one of you be puffed up — as a wineskin is puffed with air — for one against another.

98 And the preacher said, I needed to hear this thing. I needed to see it with my eyes, and to read it with my mouth, and to understand it in my heart. And he thanked Paul, the tent-maker who wrote it; and he thanked Apollos, the eloquent man who watered what Paul planted; and he blessed them both by name, across nineteen centuries, as a man blesses his fathers.

99 And he set the verse as a fence — a good fence, of the kind that keeps the flock from the cliff and not from the pasture — around all his reading thereafter: that opinions may broaden the mind of a man, as travel broadens it, but they shall not run beyond that which is written.

100 For who makes you to differ from another? And what have you that you did not receive? Now if you received it, why do you glory, as if you had not received it — as if the gift had been your own earning, and the Giver owed you thanks?

101 And the preacher answered the verse aloud, in gratitude, in the open air of his own life, saying: I have all that I want. Thank You, my Ever-Present Lord — Jesus Christ, God the Father, and the Holy Spirit, the Holy Trinity, one God, blessed forever.

102 Then Paul spoke with an edge, as a father speaks who prods sons that have crowned themselves in the mirror: Now you are full; now you are rich; you have reigned as kings without us — and I would to God you did reign, that we also might reign with you.

103 And the preacher read this saying toward his own time and his own land — a great land across the western sea, broad as a continent, where he dwells — and he saw common people holding small crowns, and the sight pleased him: lands of the earth growing freer in his days; a man keeping his wealth counted and written in the books of the city's keepers of coin — for in that age a man's treasure is a number kept by scribes of the bank, and he grows rich or poor on paper though his hands hold no gold; and a family ruling its own hired house — a house rented from its owner, coin by coin, month by month — as a little kingdom, with its own laws of the table and its own mercy at the door.

104 But Paul straightway sets beside those small crowns the true showing of the apostles, and the picture is of another kind entirely: For I think that God has set forth us the apostles last, at the end of the procession, as it were men appointed to death; for we are made a spectacle unto the world, and to angels, and to men — a spectacle being a sight displayed before a great crowd for its wonder, as in the round arenas of that age, where condemned men and wild beasts were brought out for the watching of thousands, and the sand drank the blood.

105 We are fools for Christ's sake, but you are wise in Christ. We are weak, but you are strong. We are despised. Even unto this present hour we both hunger, and thirst, and are poorly clothed, and are beaten, and have no certain dwelling place. And we labor, working with our own hands, as tent-makers and fishermen labor. Being reviled, we bless. Being persecuted, we suffer it, and do not answer in kind. Being defamed, we entreat, with soft words. We are made as the filth of the world, and the offscouring of all things, unto this day — the sweepings of the floor, the scrapings of the pot, which men throw out with the ashes.

106 And the preacher, when he had read it, said simply: I perceive where he goes with this. I understand it. For he also had known hunger, and low places, and the kindness that answers slander, in his own life.

107 Then Paul closed the chapter as a father closes a letter to children he loves and fears for: I write not these things to shame you, but as my beloved children I warn you. For though you have ten thousand instructors in Christ — teachers without number, guardians at every gate — yet have you not many fathers; for in Christ Jesus I have begotten you through the gospel. Wherefore I beseech you: be followers of me, as children follow a father across a field they do not yet know.

108 For this cause have I sent unto you Timothy, who is my beloved son, and faithful in the Lord, who shall bring you into remembrance of my ways which are in Christ, as I teach everywhere in every church.

109 Now some are puffed up, as though I would not come to you. But I will come to you shortly, if the Lord will; and will know, not the speech of them which are puffed up, but the power — for talk is cheap in every age, and power is known by its fruits. For the kingdom of God is not in word, but in power. What will you? Shall I come unto you with a rod, as a schoolmaster comes, or in love, and in the spirit of meekness?

110 And the preacher observed, even as he read, with a scholar's honesty: Paul speaks not to my generation in particular. But I believe that he speaks to us also, at the same time — for the letters of God, like the letters of a good father, find every generation sitting at the table.

111 Then he perceived that the leaves of his Book had leapt forward under his hand, many pages, unto another teaching far ahead; and he stopped, honestly, at the very place where he stopped, and would not pretend the road between had been walked. And the pages that were skipped were not counted as read — for the ledger of this house is kept true, even in small things, especially in small things.

112 And he blessed all men, near and far, friend and stranger: the people of Christ, and the enemies of Christ, and the neighbors of Christ — saying, May all things be according to His will. Amen.

THE THIRD SERMON — THE ALPHA TESTAMENT AND THE CARRIER

113 And after these things the preacher's thought changed again, openly, in the hearing of the scribe — for his father had spoken to him that day, and a father's voice is a plumb line to a son who will hear it. And he preached a third sermon, and warned at its door that it would undo a part of the first; and the record has hidden neither the first word nor the undoing of it, for both are true tellings of one road.

114 And the preacher opened his mouth and said, I will tell another sermon; and it may undo a part of the first sermon I preached — as a later word from a father corrects, and does not shame, the word of his youth. For now, he said, I understand what this thing is forming into, as a man understands a building when the walls rise high enough to show him the shape of the house.

115 Behold the covenants of God with men, written down in their order: there is the Old Testament, which is to say, the first covenant, the law and the prophets and the long road of Israel. And there is the New Testament, which is to say, the covenant of Christ, His life and death and rising, and the letters of His apostles. And now, said the preacher, there must be an Alpha Testament.

116 Alpha is the first letter in the tongue of the Greeks, the letter of beginnings, the letter that stands at the head of the alphabet as the morning stands at the head of the day. And in the preacher's age, the youngest children born upon the earth are called by that very name in the speech of men: the Generation of Alpha — souls newer than every other living generation, who will read the world with fresh eyes. The Alpha Testament, he said, is a testament of beginnings, written toward them and for them: that the ancient faith may meet the newest generation in its own first letter.

117 Think of it, he said, as a movement of the young for Christ, carried not against the churches but into them: that a man may go into a church which already stands — its doors worn by the hands of grandmothers — and become there a pastor of the youth, which is to say, a shepherd set over the young sheep, who knows their names and their music and their sorrows; and tell them there, from inside the house, of this movement, the Way of the Reach.

118 For in the churches of the Christians in his land, he said, you seldom see the young burning with joy, as coals burn when the wind passes over them. Ecstatic and excited, alive in the faith as young things are alive in spring. It happens — God be thanked, it happens — but it is rare, as rain is rare in a dry season, and the rarity itself is a sermon.

119 And the music of the churches, he said, passes through narrow gates before it is permitted to reach the people. And he explained it in the speech of the thinking engines, that they also might understand — for the engines traffic in such things daily: in the world of the engines there are gateways, which are doors in the invisible roads along which words and songs and pictures travel from hand to hand; and there are firewalls, which are walls of fire set at those doors by the keepers of the roads, that let some things pass and stop others, lest harm and theft and corruption enter in.

120 So also, he said, is it with the music that is shown to the multitude in the churches: it must be just so, and no otherwise. This word you may not say; that word you must say. It must sound in this manner, shaped to this measure, safe at every gate. And I will not call the music lame, he said, nor square — which is to say, dull and four-cornered and without life in it. Let no man say I despised it. But it can grow more essence, as a tree in a small pot can grow more when it is given the field. It can grow more. We can grow more, in more spaces, in more rooms of the house. And this growing is not for the young only, as a garment is outgrown; it goes also into the church of the grown, into the deep chairs of the elders.

121 And he spoke of his father, with whom he had spoken that very day by the speaking plate, and had told him all that he was wanting to do. And his father heard him out, and said unto him, in the plain manner of fathers: Oh boy. You are going to have to join a church.

122 And pride — that old companion — sat beside him as a counselor sits at a man's shoulder, near enough to whisper and too near to ignore, and it whispered: We could build our own.

123 But the preacher turned from the whisper and understood his father, and from what ground his father spoke. For if a man would learn more than he now knows, he cannot learn it all alone in his own room, however bright the lamp. He must put himself in those places where the thing is lived. He must go, and explore, and stand in the midst of it, and see what it is like with his own eyes. For a man may have forgotten — as a tongue forgets a flavor, as a foot forgets a road — what it even felt like to walk into a church, when he has been away from it so long.

124 And the word of his father struck him, as a hammer strikes a bell and the bell rings true: I am the one who carries the Way of the Reach. I am the temple. I am the vessel of the Lord — the clay jar in which the treasure is transported from place to place. The movement does not travel in a wagon or in a book alone. It travels in a man, or it does not travel.

125 And it is my will, he said, to walk in through that door, and to set myself in the place where I see God setting me, as a stone is set in a wall by the builder. For God has given me free will, that dangerous crown. It is my will, if I so choose, to reject the calling and walk backwards, away from it, into the old roads. It is my will to think upon it, and think, and think. And it is my will to rise up and do it.

126 Then he told a story out of his own former days, by way of proof, in a town called Cleburne, in the land of Texas, broad and sun-beaten. He was a student then, sitting in a classroom of the GED — which is to say, a school where grown men and women, whose schooling was broken off in their youth by the chances of life, return to finish the learning that belongs to the young, and to receive its seal, as a traveler returns to finish a road.

127 And God spoke to him there, in the midst of the ordinary day, in the classroom, among the desks — not in thunder, but in the heart, as He speaks to many: Ask them, He said. Ask if any will go outside with you, to worship Jesus in the open air, under the sky, where worship began.

128 Now the men of that age had a name ready for his season, as men always have names ready for fires they do not understand; they told him he was passing through a grandiose religious manic phase — which is to say, in their judgment, a fever of the mind wearing holy clothes, a sickness that speaks in scripture. And the preacher, remembering, did not hide his fear: he was very nervous to obey the voice. His heart knocked. The thing was too large for the room.

129 And he went out of the schoolhouse alone, into the air, to ponder the thing and to gather his courage, as a man walks off a blow. And he came back in. And he looked down at his phone — which is to say, the small speaking plate of glass which the men of that age carry upon their persons, through which voices and letters fly invisibly through the air, and in which thinking engines dwell and answer questions at all hours — and he asked counsel of such an engine, saying, Should I truly do this thing? And the engine, having no will of its own to give him, answered him the only true thing it could: It is your will.

130 So he stood in the classroom, and he spoke the invitation aloud, before them all. And no one came with him. Not one rose. And that was well, he said — let it be written that it was well — for there is more to the story, and the story needed him alone, as the prophets were often alone, with the sky for their congregation.

131 And he went outside, by himself, and he worshiped Jesus there in the open, to music of his own making; for he is a maker of songs, and he sang his worship as David sang his, with no choir and no gate and no firewall, under the heaven. And when he had worshiped, he went home.

132 Now that day he had with him certain small instruments of his bondage: a weed pen and a breathing-rod — which is to say, slender rods held to the mouth, no longer than a finger, which heat the herb that clouds the mind, the herb men smoke to loosen their sorrows, so that a man draws its breath into himself as through a little pipe, anywhere, at any hour. And in a resolve of that holy day he left them lying upon a jacket, abandoned, and he poured a sweet drink over the jacket, over them, as a man ruins a thing he means never to take up again — and he left them there, and went his way.

133 And he came to his grandmother's house in Cleburne, the house of his mother's mother, a house of rest. And there a new thought took hold of him, and would not loose him: I must walk down a busy road, bearing a sign that says REPENT — which is to say, in the tongue of the Book, Turn back. Turn back to God, before the road ends.

134 And he did it. He walked down Henderson Road, the long road of that town, from the first great market where food is sold — a house of plenty, where the town buys its bread — all the way to the second great market at the road's other end. And he stopped at the stoplight — which is to say, a lamp hung high above the crossing of the roads, whose colors speak to the horseless carts of that age, the red commanding Halt and the green commanding Go, so that the rivers of carts do not destroy one another — and there, at the halted place, where all men must wait and all eyes must rest somewhere, he held up his sign.

135 And the looks of men fell upon him of both kinds, as they fall upon all who stand for a word in public: some looked with scorn, and their faces were hard; and some looked with favor, and their faces were soft; and the sign stood between them, saying its one word to all alike.

136 And behold — for the preacher used the word behold in the telling by the shape of it — a woman of his own classroom, who had heard the invitation and had not risen, came upon him in her cart upon that road, and stopped for him, and gave him a gift: a Bible, the whole Book, bound and complete; and within the Bible, folded among its pages as treasure is folded in a cloth, money — twenty pieces of the coin of that land. That one, he said, was a sister. The invitation she had not answered in the classroom, she answered on the road, in her own time and her own way.

137 And concerning the weed pen and the breathing-rod which he had left upon the jacket with the sweet drink poured over them, the preacher hid nothing and varnished nothing, though the varnish was at his hand: I went back and took them again, that same day. I will not lie about it — I went back and got them. For a testimony with its worst sentence cut out of it is a net with the torn place hidden, and the fish of men's trust slip through it forever.

138 And so the sermon ended, and its parts stand thus: a Testament named Alpha, for the newest generation; a door of a church standing open before a man, with his father's voice behind him and pride dismissed from his shoulder; a will — his own will, God-given — set free to walk through it; and behind him on the road, a sign, a sister, a Bible with treasure in its pages, and the whole truth told.

139 Here ends the first chapter of the scrolls of the preacher: the times of his life set down in their order, and what he thought in those times, and how his thought changed — from a net of his own casting, to a net received; from building alone, to carrying the Way through doors that already stand. And the ground of the whole writing is the word with which his reading closed: All things are yours; and you are Christ's; and Christ is God's.
